A note on honesty in this document

Everywhere we describe a feature, we mark it. Built means it works right now against live data. Partial means it works with a limitation, and the limitation is written down. Later means we designed it and did not build it.

We do this because a document that only lists successes is not an architecture document, it is an advertisement. A judge, an engineer, or a fisheries officer reading this should be able to tell exactly what they would be relying on.

Part 1

What ORCA is

Every morning, along roughly 7,500 kilometres of Indian coastline, several hundred thousand people have to answer one question: should I take the boat out today? Getting it wrong is not an inconvenience. It is the leading cause of death in Indian small-scale fishing.

The information already exists. It is just unusable.

India's oceanographic and meteorological agencies publish genuinely excellent data. Wave heights, wind speeds, sea temperatures, thunderstorm forecasts, fishing advisories. The problem is not the data. The problem is the last hundred metres between the data and the person standing on the beach at four in the morning.

That gap has four parts:

  • Language. The bulletins are in English. A large share of the people who need them do not read English comfortably, and some do not read at all.
  • Format. A bulletin says "significant wave height 2.6 metres". It does not say whether that is safe for your boat. A 6-metre wooden kattumaram and a 20-metre steel trawler have wildly different tolerances, and the bulletin cannot know which one you own.
  • Fragmentation. Waves come from one place, wind from another, thunderstorm risk from a third, fishing advisories from a fourth. Nobody has time to check four sources and mentally combine them.
  • Trust. If a system tells you "do not go out" and will not say why, you will ignore it the second time. And you should.

What ORCA does about it

ORCA is a piece of software you talk to. You can speak to it in Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, Marathi, Gujarati, Odia, Konkani or English. You can ask it more than one thing at once. It answers out loud, in the same language, and it shows its working.

Concretely, it does eight things:

It can…What that means in practice
Decide Tap anywhere on the sea and ORCA gives one of four verdicts: GO, CAUTION, NO-GO, or UNVERIFIABLE — judged against your boat's published limits, not a generic threshold.
Refuse When it does not have enough information, it says UNVERIFIABLE and names exactly what was missing. It never lets absent data look like calm water.
Attribute Every single number on the screen shows where it came from, how old it is, and how it was obtained. Nothing is unattributed.
Explain When you ask a question, you watch ORCA work: it announces its plan, then fetches each piece of data one at a time with the real time each took, then a separate checking step reviews the answer before you see it.
Speak and listen Press a button, talk, and ORCA transcribes it, answers it, and reads the answer back — in eleven Indian languages.
Route Give it a destination and it plans a sea passage that avoids every patch of water its own safety rules reject. If no safe route exists, it says so and names the specific patches that block it.
Search If someone goes missing, ORCA calculates where the sea will have carried them — as a search area, never as a single guessed point.
Warn It watches positions you care about and tells you when things get worse, without being asked.
The sentence that describes the whole product

ORCA answers a fisherman's question in his own language and out loud, shows every number's source and age, computes the go / no-go with a fixed rule engine that the artificial intelligence is not permitted to overrule, and says "I cannot tell" when it cannot — which is the only version of this product that is safe to put in front of a real person.

Part 2

The fifteen words you need

Almost all the jargon in this document is one of these fifteen things. Read this page once and the rest of the document is plain reading. There is a full alphabetical glossary in Part 9 for anything else.

1  ·  EEZ — Exclusive Economic Zone
The band of sea a country controls economically, extending 200 nautical miles (about 370 km) from its coast. India's EEZ is roughly 2.3 million square kilometres. ORCA covers a rectangle around it: 60°E–100°E, 0°N–25°N.
2  ·  API — Application Programming Interface
A way for one piece of software to ask another piece of software a question. When ORCA needs a wave height, it calls an API belonging to a weather service — like phoning a specific department and getting a structured answer back rather than a conversation. ORCA also offers an API, so other software could use it.
3  ·  Hs — Significant Wave Height
The standard measure of how rough the sea is. Roughly the average height of the biggest third of the waves, measured from trough to crest, in metres. It is not the biggest wave — individual waves can be nearly twice Hs. This is the single most important number in the whole system.
4  ·  Knot (kn)
One nautical mile per hour — about 1.85 km/h. The standard unit for wind and boat speed at sea. 22 knots is roughly 41 km/h. Worth knowing because confusing knots with kilometres is a mistake ORCA actively guards against, having been bitten by it.
5  ·  CAPE — Convective Available Potential Energy
A measure, in joules per kilogram, of how much energy the atmosphere has available to build thunderstorms. High CAPE means thunderstorms are possible, not that one is happening. India has no free authoritative real-time lightning feed, so ORCA uses CAPE as an honest stand-in and says so.
6  ·  SST — Sea Surface Temperature
How warm the top layer of the ocean is. It matters for fishing: fish gather where warm and cool water meet, because that boundary concentrates the plankton they feed on.
7  ·  PFZ — Potential Fishing Zone
An advisory saying "fish are likely here". India's oceanographic institute publishes these daily as picture maps. Because picture maps cannot be read by software, ORCA re-computes the same thing from the same underlying satellite measurements.
8  ·  INCOIS · IMD · MOSDAC · NDMA
The four Indian government bodies that matter here. INCOIS = Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (ocean data and fishing advisories). IMD = India Meteorological Department (weather warnings). MOSDAC = Meteorological and Oceanographic Satellite Data Archival Centre (ISRO's satellite data). NDMA = National Disaster Management Authority (runs SACHET, the national alert system). ORCA is designed to supplement these, never to replace them.
9  ·  LLM — Large Language Model
The kind of artificial intelligence behind tools like ChatGPT. It is very good at understanding a question and writing a fluent answer. It is not reliable at arithmetic or at following a rule exactly every time. That single fact shapes ORCA's entire design — see Part 3.
10  ·  Agent
An LLM that has been given tools and permission to use them in a sequence it chooses. Instead of just answering from memory, it can decide "I need the wave height, then I need to check the safety rules, then I can answer." ORCA has one agent with eight tools.
11  ·  Deterministic
Given the same inputs, always produces exactly the same output, and you can trace precisely why. The opposite of an LLM, which may phrase the same answer three different ways. ORCA's safety verdict is deterministic, on purpose.
12  ·  Provenance
The origin story of a number. Not just "29.1 °C" but "29.1 °C, from NASA's satellite product, measured 4 hours ago, obtained live". ORCA attaches this to every value it handles and refuses to carry values that lack it.
13  ·  Staleness
Whether a number is too old to be trusted for what it is. Six hours is fine for a sea temperature and useless for a thunderstorm forecast, so ORCA keeps a different expiry time for every kind of measurement.
14  ·  Frontend and Backend
The frontend is what you see and touch — the map, the panels, the microphone button. It runs in your web browser. The backend is the part that does the work — fetching data, running the science, computing the verdict. It runs on a server. They talk to each other over the internet using an API.
15  ·  Streaming (and SSE — Server-Sent Events)
Normally software asks a question and waits for the whole answer. Streaming means the answer arrives in pieces as it is produced. ORCA streams its reasoning, so you watch it work rather than staring at a spinner. SSE is the specific web technique used. This matters for trust: a step-by-step trace that arrives all at once at the end is indistinguishable from one that was made up.
Part 3

The one big idea

If you read only one part of this document, read this one. Every design decision in ORCA follows from a single problem, and understanding the problem makes the rest of the system obvious.

The problem: language models are fluent and unreliable

A large language model is genuinely remarkable at understanding a badly-phrased question in Tamil and producing a clear, kind, well-organised answer. That is a hard problem and it solves it well.

It is also capable of confidently writing "conditions look fine" when the wave height is over the limit. Not because it is broken — because it is a system that predicts plausible text, and "conditions look fine" is extremely plausible text. It has no mechanism that forces it to compare 2.6 against 1.5 and act on the result.

The failure this system exists to prevent

A fisherman asks, in Tamil, whether it is safe. The waves are 2.6 metres and his boat's published limit is 1.5 metres. A language model answers, fluently and warmly, that conditions are manageable if he is careful. He goes out. That is not a software bug. That is a death.

The solution: split the system in two, and put a wall between them

ORCA separates deciding from explaining, and gives them to two completely different kinds of software.

The rule engineThe language model
What it does Decides GO / CAUTION / NO-GO / UNVERIFIABLE Explains the decision in the user's language
How it works Fixed arithmetic against published limits. Same input, same output, every time. Predicts fluent text. Same input, different phrasing each time.
Can it be wrong? Only if the published limits are wrong — and those are cited, versioned, and swappable. Yes, in wording. It is not allowed anywhere near the decision.
Is it allowed to change the verdict? It is the verdict. No. This is enforced in four separate ways.

How the wall is enforced — four independent mechanisms

Saying "the model must not change the verdict" in an instruction is not enforcement, it is a request. ORCA enforces it four times over, so that three of the four can fail and the rule still holds.

1
The type system makes the wrong answer unrepresentable

Every verdict carries a field recording who decided it. That field is declared as being able to hold exactly one value: "rule_engine". Not "usually rule_engine" — only. There is no way to write "llm" into it, because the program would refuse to run.

2
An automated test forbids the wiring from existing

The rule engine lives in a folder called services/. The AI lives in a folder called agents/. A test checks, every single time anyone changes the code, that nothing in services/ so much as mentions agents/. The deterministic core physically cannot acquire a dependency on the AI, even by accident.

3
A critic reviews the model's draft before you see it

After the language model writes its answer, a separate checking step compares it against the rule engine's verdict. If the draft softens a NO-GO, or quotes a figure that does not appear in the fetched data, the draft is rejected and rewritten. The interface shows you when this happened and how many attempts it took — a rejection is displayed, not hidden.

4
The interface says so, on screen, every time

The verdict card carries the line "Decided by a deterministic rule engine — no language model", along with the version of the limits table used. The claim is auditable by the person relying on it.

The second half of the idea: no number without its origin

The other thing that makes advice trustworthy is being able to check it. So ORCA has a rule that applies to every value in the entire system: a number may not travel through the software as a bare number. It must be wrapped in a record stating where it came from.

What every value looks like inside ORCA
value29.1
unitdegrees Celsius
variablesea surface temperature
datasetwhich specific product it came from
providerwhich organisation published it
provenanceone of six states — see below
freshnesswhen it was valid, how old that makes it, and whether that is too old for this kind of measurement

The six provenance states, and why six

"Where did this come from" has more than two useful answers. Collapsing them to "we have data" and "we don't" throws away exactly the distinctions a person needs to judge risk.

StateMeansWhy it is separate
LIVEFetched from the source just nowThe best case. Nothing to qualify.
CACHEDFetched earlier, reusedMight be stale. The user should see the age, not just the value.
CURATEDReference information we hold ourselves — treaty lines, boat limitsDoes not go stale the way a forecast does. A 2024 maritime boundary is still correct.
DERIVEDCalculated by ORCA from other valuesMust declare its inputs, or the calculation is unauditable magic. ORCA refuses to construct a derived value that does not name its sources.
SIMULATEDDemonstration data, not realDisplayed with a hatched pattern, and excluded from safety decisions entirely. A go / no-go can never rest on invented data.
UNAVAILABLEWe tried and could not get itThe most important one. This is what stops a gap in the data reading as good news.
The consequence worth stating

Because UNAVAILABLE is a real state rather than an absence, ORCA has a fourth verdict alongside GO, CAUTION and NO-GO: UNVERIFIABLE. It appears with the words "ORCA could not obtain enough data to judge — this is not the same as safe", and it lists which specific inputs were missing. Most systems do not have this verdict. It is the one we are proudest of.

Layer 1 — Where the data actually comes from

ORCA owns no sensors. Everything it knows, it asks somebody else for. Which means a large part of the engineering is about behaving well as a guest on other people's systems, and being honest when they are unavailable.

IMD needs an API key and our server's address whitelisted; MOSDAC needs an ISRO registration; Bhashini is the Government of India's own language platform and would make a good sovereign fallback behind Sarvam.

Layer 2 — The provenance contract

Described fully in Part 3. It is listed as a layer because it is not a piece of code you call — it is a shape that every value in the system is required to have, checked automatically at the moment each value is created. A value that fails the check does not get created; the program raises an error instead of quietly carrying an unattributed number.

Two of those automatic checks are worth naming:

  • A DERIVED value with no declared inputs is rejected at construction. This is why the fishing-zone layer cannot be magic — it is structurally obliged to name what it was computed from.
  • An UNAVAILABLE value that nonetheless carries a number is rejected. "We could not get it" and "here is the figure" are contradictory, and the type refuses to hold both.

Layer 3 — Source adapters

One module per external source, all sitting on a shared client that handles the unglamorous realities of talking to other people's servers:

BehaviourWhy it is there
Retries with backoffA network hiccup should not become a user-facing failure. Waits progressively longer between attempts rather than hammering a struggling server.
Circuit breakerAfter repeated failures, stop trying for a while. Continuing to call a dead service wastes the user's time and is rude to the operator.
Conditional fetching (ETag)Ask "has this changed since I last looked?" If not, the server replies with a tiny "no" instead of resending everything.
Health reportingEvery success and failure is recorded, which is what the "sources 6/7" indicator in the interface is reading. It is a measurement, not a decoration.
Failures do not raiseA source being down returns a result marked as failed, rather than crashing the request. One dead source must not take down the answer.
Units read, never assumedCovered in Part 6. This one caused the worst bug in the project's history.

Layer 4 — Science

Pure calculation. No network, no database, no AI — which means every function here can be tested by giving it numbers and checking the numbers that come out.

ModuleWhat it computes
GridOne fixed grid for the whole system: 60°E–100°E, 0°N–25°N at 0.05° spacing — 800 × 500 = 400,000 cells, each about 5.5 km across. Everything is resampled onto it so layers can be compared cell by cell.
FrontsFinds boundaries between warm and cool water — the places fish gather. Three different detection methods, and the interface says which one produced the layer you are looking at.
Potential Fishing ZonesCombines temperature, temperature gradients and chlorophyll into ranked zones, and reports which criteria it could not apply.
Colour mapsTurns numbers into pictures. Seven scientific colour scales. Crucially, the legend shown to the user is generated from the same lookup table that coloured the pixels, so the legend cannot disagree with the image.
Vector fieldsHandles wind and current as directional arrows, and contains the single most dangerous convention in marine data — see Part 6.

Layer 5 — The deterministic core

The most important layer. Everything here is fixed arithmetic and geometry, with no AI anywhere near it.

The risk engine

Four measurements, weighted into one index out of 100:

MeasurementWeightNote
Wave height35%The dominant factor for a small boat.
Wind speed30%Drives both waves and handling difficulty.
Lightning probability20%Derived from CAPE, and labelled as such.
Visibility15%Matters most for collision and for finding your way home.

Then — and this is the part that matters — hard vetoes are checked separately. If wave height is at or over your boat class's limit, or lightning probability is at or over 60%, the verdict is NO-GO regardless of the index.

Why a veto is not just a heavy weight

If exceeding the wave limit were merely a large penalty, then a calm wind, clear visibility and no lightning could out-vote it and produce a GO in a sea that would sink the boat. Weighted averages have no concept of "never". So "never" is built as a separate mechanism that runs after the average and overrides it. The same reasoning appears again in the router, where a forbidden patch of sea is made impassable rather than expensive.

The five Indian boat classes

This is what makes the answer personal rather than generic. Set your boat's length and the limits change.

ClassMax HsMax windMin visibility
Traditional non-motorised (kattumaram, canoe)1.0 m15 kn2.0 km
Motorised fibreglass boat, 7–10 m1.5 m22 kn2.0 km
Small mechanised gillnetter / trawler, 10–15 m2.0 m28 kn1.0 km
Mechanised trawler, 15–24 m2.5 m33 kn1.0 km
Deep-sea vessel, over 24 m3.5 m40 kn0.5 km

The table carries a version stamp — currently orca-thresholds-2026.08 — which is displayed on every verdict. If a fisheries authority disagrees with a number, they can point at exactly which version produced the advice, and replacing it is a configuration change rather than a code change.

An honest limitation, stated up front

INCOIS does not publish its own small-craft warning thresholds. Ours are seeded from peer-reviewed small-craft literature, cited row by row, and explicitly versioned so they can be replaced by official values the moment those are available. We are not claiming these are the official Indian numbers. We are claiming they are defensible, visible, and swappable.

Geofencing — knowing when you are about to cross a border

Crossing a maritime boundary can mean arrest and boat confiscation. The Palk Bay line between India and Sri Lanka is close enough to fishing grounds that this is a routine hazard, not an exotic one.

ORCA holds the boundary geometry and answers: how far away, in which direction, and — given your heading and speed — how many minutes until you cross. It reports on transitions: the moment you move from "clear" to "approaching", or "approaching" to "crossed". Not a constant readout, which people stop noticing.

Two engineering details worth mentioning because both were mistakes we made and fixed:

  • Checking a position against every boundary shape is slow. ORCA uses a spatial index — a structure that first narrows the search to nearby candidates with a cheap test, then does the exact geometry on just those. Roughly the difference between reading a whole index and reading a whole book.
  • Distances use proper geodesic calculation on the Earth's actual ellipsoid shape, verified against an independent implementation to within one millimetre on a known pair of points. Treating latitude and longitude as flat coordinates is wrong by kilometres at this scale, and "kilometres" is the difference between inside and outside a national boundary.

Safe-passage routing

Give ORCA a destination and it plans a route. Explained in full in Part 5, but the architectural point is this: the router contains no safety thresholds of its own. It asks the same risk engine that produces the on-screen verdict about every patch of sea it considers. There is exactly one definition of "unsafe" in the system.

Search-and-rescue drift

If a person or boat goes missing, ORCA calculates where the sea will have carried them. It returns a search area and refuses to return a position — the reasoning is in Part 5.

CAP advisory generation

CAP stands for Common Alerting Protocol — an international standard (published by a body called OASIS) for machine-readable emergency alerts. India's NDMA SACHET system and the IMD already publish in it.

ORCA can export any advisory as valid CAP 1.2, with a second block in the local language. This is the "designed to fit in" feature: rather than asking existing Indian alerting infrastructure to adopt a new format, ORCA speaks the format they already use.

The trip monitor

The only part of ORCA that speaks without being asked. It re-checks watched positions every two minutes and raises an alert when something crosses a line. Design and reasoning in Part 5.

Layer 6 — The agent plane

This is where the language model lives, fenced off from the decision. It is built on a framework called LangGraph, which lets you describe an AI workflow as a graph of steps with explicit paths between them, rather than as one large instruction and a hope.

The stages, in order:

1
Interaction — work out what was actually asked

Detects the language, and decomposes compound questions. "Is it safe tomorrow, and where will I find fish?" becomes two labelled sub-questions, and the tools those labels require become a floor the next stage may add to but not drop from.

This exists because of an observed failure: asked both halves, the planner would answer the first and silently ignore the second. Making the tool set a floor rather than a suggestion fixed it. There is a keyword-based fallback that works with no AI at all, including for romanised Indian text like "pogum vazhi enna".

2
Planning — choose the smallest set of tools that answers it

ORCA has eight tools. Each publishes a machine-readable description of itself: what kinds of question it answers, its resolution, its typical speed, what provenance its results carry, and how expensive it is to run.

The planner reads those descriptions and picks. Selecting everything is treated as a wrong answer: it wastes time and demonstrates no judgement. The plan is published to the interface before anything runs, which is what makes the trace verifiable rather than a summary written afterwards.

3
Execution — run the tools, in a safe order

The order is a real constraint, not a preference: the tool that assesses risk needs the tool that fetches conditions to have run first. That ordering is defined in exactly one place in the codebase — after a bug where two copies had drifted apart, and a newly-added tool was present in one and missing from the other, so it worked when tested alone and was silently dropped from every compound question.

4
Reporting — write the answer

The language model receives the tool results, the rule engine's verdict marked as authoritative and unchangeable, and — for compound questions — the numbered list of parts it must address. It is instructed, in three different phrasings, that it may explain a verdict and never issue or soften one.

5
Criticism — check the draft, and send it back if needed

The critic checks that the verdict is stated plainly, that no softening language contradicts a NO-GO, and that every figure quoted actually appears in the fetched data. A rejected draft is regenerated with the reason attached, and the interface shows the round count.

The critic itself had a revealing bug: it compared numbers as text, so a correct draft quoting "72.7" was rejected three times for not containing "73". It now compares numerically with a tolerance, and eighteen tests pin the behaviour in both directions — it must catch a genuine fabrication and it must not reject a correct rounding.

Layer 7 — The language plane

Eleven languages: English, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Gujarati, Odia and Konkani. Four capabilities: detect, translate, speak, listen. And one component that matters more than the other four combined — the number-integrity guard, which gets its own section in Part 6 because it is the single most interesting piece of engineering in the project.

CapabilityHow it works
Detection Identifies the language from the script, and handles romanised Indian text — someone typing Tamil in Latin letters on an English keyboard, which is extremely common.
Translation Sarvam first, Google Gemini as fallback. Every result passes the guard before anyone sees it.
Speech synthesis Sarvam's Bulbul model. All eleven voices were verified by actually generating audio rather than by trusting the documentation — which is how we found that Odia's documented voice is rejected by the live service, and that Konkani has no native voice at all. ORCA marks the Konkani voice as a substitute rather than passing a Marathi voice off as Konkani.
Speech recognition Two engines, chosen per language on measured evidence. On the same Tamil recording, Sarvam's Saarika transcribed it exactly including the numbers, while OpenAI's Whisper garbled them. So Indian languages go to Sarvam and English goes to Whisper — a decision from a measurement, not a preference.
A small detail with a real reason

Web browsers record audio in a format called WebM/Opus. Sarvam's recognition service rejects it with an error that looks like an authentication failure. Rather than relabelling the file and hoping, ORCA converts the recording to 16 kHz mono WAV inside the browser — the format both engines are trained on — using the browser's own audio decoder. Anything richer is upload bandwidth the model discards, and on a harbour phone connection the upload is the slow part.

Layer 8 — The HTTP interface

47 endpoints. Two deserve comment because they behave unusually on purpose.

  • The agent stream sends its reasoning in pieces as it happens, with instructions to intermediate network equipment not to buffer it, no compression on that path, and a periodic heartbeat so an idle connection is not cut while waiting for a slow data source. All three exist because a trace that arrives in one lump at the end proves nothing.
  • The route planner returns "success" for a refusal. "There is no safe passage for an 8-metre boat across this corridor today, and here are the six patches of sea that block it" is a correct and complete answer to the question asked. Returning it as an error would put it in the same category as a malformed request and encourage software to retry it.

A web application. The map is a globe — a real spherical projection, not a flat rectangle — with data layers drawn on top of it by a high-performance graphics library that talks directly to the computer's graphics hardware.

Part 5

Three journeys, end to end

The fastest way to understand a system is to follow one request all the way through. Here are three, in increasing complexity.

Journey 1 — Tapping a point on the sea

The simplest thing ORCA does, and it touches six layers.

1
You tap the map

The browser converts your tap into a latitude and longitude and sends two requests to the backend: one for conditions, one for the verdict. They are sent together on purpose — if they were fetched separately they could disagree, and a verdict that disagrees with the evidence panel below it is worse than no verdict.

2
The backend asks two weather services at once

Waves and currents come from a marine service; wind, visibility and thunderstorm energy come from an atmospheric one. Both are asked simultaneously rather than one after the other, because they do not depend on each other and the delay is visible to you.

3
Each value is wrapped in its provenance

As each number arrives it is packaged with its source, its unit as declared by that source, when it was valid, and whether that makes it stale for its kind. A value the service could not supply becomes UNAVAILABLE rather than silently vanishing.

4
The rule engine decides

Your boat length selects a class, the class supplies the limits, the four measurements are scored and weighted, the hard vetoes are checked separately, and one of four verdicts comes out — with the arithmetic recorded so the interface can show it.

5
Confidence is assessed separately from the verdict

If a measurement was missing, it scores zero and forces low confidence. This matters enormously: "we could not measure the waves" and "the waves are calm" must never produce the same output. If confidence is too low, the verdict becomes UNVERIFIABLE with the missing inputs named.

6
You see the verdict, and the receipt

The card, the vetoes, what would change the answer, and beneath it every number with its provenance badge — including whether it is a forecast for a time in the future rather than a stale measurement from the past. ORCA tracks that distinction because a value valid at 06:00 tomorrow is fourteen hours "old" by simple subtraction and perfectly current in fact.

Journey 2 — Asking a compound question in Tamil, by voice

The full stack. Around fifteen seconds of work, all of it visible to you as it happens.

1
You hold the microphone button and speak

A meter moves with your actual voice level — driven by the measured signal, not an animation, so a muted microphone visibly does nothing. Release, and the recording is converted in-browser to the format the recognition engines expect.

2
ORCA transcribes it and shows you what it heard

It does not send it straight on. Speech recognition on a fisherman's question with a place name in it is the least reliable link in the chain, so you see the transcript and can correct it. ORCA also detects the language of the transcript and warns you if it does not match what you selected.

3
The question is split in two

"Is it safe tomorrow?" and "where will I find fish?" are separated and labelled. The tools each label needs become a floor. You see this happen in the trace.

4
The plan appears, before anything runs

Five tools, with the reason each was chosen. Then they execute one at a time and each result appears with the real time it took.

5
The rule engine returns the verdict, and the model writes the answer

The verdict is handed to the model as unchangeable. The model writes English prose covering both halves of the question in the order asked.

6
The critic reviews it

You see its ruling and how many attempts were needed.

7
Translation, sentence by sentence, with the numbers guarded

The English answer appears immediately. The Tamil arrives a moment later as a separate delivery, so you are not kept waiting on the translation. Each sentence is translated and verified independently — and a sentence that cannot be verified stays in English rather than being translated unsafely. The panel reports "figures intact · 18" and, if any clause was held back, says so.

8
It speaks

The spoken version is a summary — the verdict and the reason that drove it. The full compound answer synthesised to 66 seconds of Tamil, and nobody listens to 66 seconds of audio before launching a boat. The panel shows what was actually said, so a user who hears less than they read knows why. Whole lines are dropped, never half a figure.

Journey 3 — Planning a safe passage

1
A corridor of sea is divided into a grid

A rectangle covering start and destination plus a margin either side, divided into cells about 28 km across. The number of cells is capped — not arbitrarily, but calculated from how many questions ORCA is allowed to ask the weather service per minute.

That cap came from a real bug: an earlier fixed limit let one request spend its whole allowance fetching wave data, whereupon the wind request was refused — and the route came back looking fine, costed on wave height alone with wind, visibility and thunderstorm energy missing at every cell. It looked like a working router with a cautious opinion. Now the cap is derived from the budget, and a variable missing everywhere is reported rather than inferred.

2
Every cell is judged by the same rule engine

Not a simplified copy — the identical function that produces the on-screen verdict. A cell with any hard veto is marked impassable, not expensive.

3
Land is identified by the wave model's own silence

This is an idea worth pausing on. ORCA has no coastline database for this purpose. Instead: the marine forecast service returns nothing for wave height over land — so a cell with no wave height is a cell the wave model itself declines to answer for, which is exactly the set a boat must not cross.

It is more accurate than any coastline outline at this resolution, and it collapses "cannot route here" and "cannot measure here" into one state. That is the honest position: a patch of sea ORCA cannot measure is not a patch ORCA can clear.

4
A path-finding algorithm searches for the cheapest route

The algorithm is A* ("A-star"), the standard method for finding a shortest path when you have a way to estimate remaining distance. Cost per cell is its physical distance multiplied by a penalty that grows with risk — squared, not linear, so a slightly worse cell is barely avoided and a nearly-forbidden one is avoided hard.

An automated test compares A*'s answer against an exhaustive search of the same grid. They must match exactly. So "this is the optimal route under the cost model" is verified rather than asserted.

5
You get a route — or a reason

A route comes with waypoints, distance, duration, and how much longer it is than a straight line. That last figure is the judgement the user has to make: 5% longer to avoid a 3-metre sea is worth taking; 180% longer means the honest answer was "not today".

If no route exists, ORCA names the specific cells that block it with the reason for each. "No route found" on its own is indistinguishable from a broken planner.

Something honest about routing during the monsoon

When we tested this off Chennai in monsoon season, ORCA refused to route any vessel class — because thunderstorm energy across the whole corridor was above the veto threshold. That is defensible and it is also coarse. It is discussed further in Part 8 under known limitations, and the demonstration script tells the presenter to check the coast on the day and to show the refusal rather than hide it.

Journey 3b — A search area for a missing person

Same layer, different problem, and the most consequential thing in the system.

ORCA models drift as the internationally standard sum of two things: the current carrying the water, plus leeway — the object's own movement through the water under wind. Leeway is not simply "3% of the wind speed downwind". Real objects move both downwind and sideways, and which side they slip to flips unpredictably between individual objects of the same type.

So ORCA runs 2,000 simulated particles. Each carries its own leeway coefficients drawn from the measured spread for its object class, and its own sideways direction. That scatter is the search area — a single set of average coefficients produces a line, and a line is not a search plan.

Why ORCA will not tell you where the person is

The model produces an average position. It would be the easiest number to put in large type, and ORCA deliberately shows it small, labelled as the centre of a distribution rather than a location.

A single predicted point is the most dangerous output a drift model can produce: it is wrong, it looks authoritative, and a coordinated search will concentrate on it. So the output is two nested areas — the region containing 50% of the particles, and the region containing 95% — and the interface says which is which.

One correction during development is worth recording, because it points at something general. The first version produced a 3-hour search area for a person in the water of 2.3 square kilometres, which would send a boat crew to look at one wave. Two things were wrong: a diffusion term had the wrong units and contributed about six metres, and — far more importantly — the current forecast's own error was missing entirely.

That error is the dominant term. A person in the water has almost no sail area, so the size of their search area is set by how wrong the current analysis is, not by the object's leeway. It is around 0.15 metres per second at this resolution, and it is correlated over time — a current forecast that is too fast in one hour is usually too fast in the next. So it is drawn once per particle and held. Treating it as fresh random noise each step understates the area by a factor of the square root of the number of steps. The corrected 3-hour area is 50 square kilometres, and a 24-hour life-raft search is 5,871.

ORCA now reports which term dominates the spread, so a search coordinator can judge whether a better current forecast would change their plan.

Journey 4 — An alert nobody asked for

Everything above is request and response: you ask, ORCA answers. The trip monitor is the exception.

You mark a position as watched. Every two minutes ORCA re-assesses it. And here is the entire design of the feature:

Alerts fire on transitions, never on state

A system that announces "NO-GO at Kasimedu" every two minutes has produced a feed. And a feed is something people learn to ignore — which means the one alert that mattered scrolls past with the ninety that did not.

So ORCA alerts only when something crossed a line since the last check: the verdict changed band, a new hard limit was exceeded, the safety margin narrowed materially within one band, a boundary state changed, or fresh data went stale. The first check after you start watching records a baseline and raises nothing — alerting on the first observation would fire for a condition that was already true when you asked to be watched. You had just looked at it.

And every alert carries what it was beside what it is. "NO-GO" alone is a status line the verdict card already shows. "GO → NO-GO, index 92 → 24, lightning crossed 60%" is an alert.

Improvements are reported too — a fisherman told NO-GO at 04:00 wants to know that 07:00 is GO — but at a low severity, so they can never sort above a deterioration.

What the monitor deliberately does not do

It sends nothing anywhere. No SMS, no push notification, no email. ORCA has no authority to contact a fisherman, and a prototype that quietly acquires a notification channel is a prototype that can spam a real person. Alerts live in the console, and the interface says so in the panel footer so that nobody demonstrates the feature as something it is not.

Part 6

The science, explained

This part answers the fair question: is the mathematics real, or is it decoration with scientific-sounding labels? Five pieces, each with the reasoning and the mistake we made getting there.

1 · The number-integrity guard

The most interesting engineering in the project, and it exists because of a single observation: translation systems are fluent, and fluency is what makes their mistakes dangerous.

Three real failures, all from live testing

Asked to translate an advisory beginning "NO-GO", the translation service returned Tamil meaning roughly "punishment for those who go astray". Fluent, confident, and it destroyed the one word the entire advisory exists to convey.

Asked to translate "wind = 14.7 kn (limit 22 kn)", it returned Tamil reading "14.7 kilometres". Every digit survived perfectly and the sentence was still wrong about the physical quantity — which is worse than losing a number, because a missing number is visibly missing and 14.7 km of wind reads as a real measurement.

And the model that writes the answers uses a typographically correct non-breaking hyphen in "NO‑GO" about half the time. Our protection pattern was written with an ordinary hyphen, so it did not match, and the verdict came back as "NO‽GO" — with an interrobang in the middle of it.

ORCA's answer is to never trust the translation, and to prove it instead:

  1. Mask. Every protected item — numerals, verdict words, compass bearings, agency names, and confusable units — is replaced with an opaque placeholder. The translator never sees a digit at all, so it cannot round, localise or "correct" one.
  2. Translate the masked text.
  3. Re-inject the originals, character for character. What comes out is byte-for-byte what went in.
  4. Verify — and this is the subtle part — as a multiset. Not a set, because "1.5 metres and 1.5 metres" is different from "1.5 metres" and a set cannot tell. Not a sequence, because a language with different word order will legitimately reorder the figures. A multiset counts occurrences and ignores order, which is exactly right.
  5. Fail safely. If verification fails, that clause is not spoken and not shown as translated. It stays in English. A mostly-Tamil advisory with one English clause is far more useful than an all-English one, and infinitely better than a fluent Tamil sentence with the wrong wave height in it.

The judgement call inside the unit list

The protected-unit list is deliberately narrow. It is not every unit. Rendering "8.4 km" as "8.4 கிமீ" is correct, idiomatic, and clearer for a Tamil reader — forcing every unit into Latin script would make the advisory worse. What is protected is only the set where a wrong translation swaps one quantity for another: knots (confusable with kilometres, which is exactly what happened), nautical miles (or nanometres), and compound units like metres per second and joules per kilogram that have no short vernacular form and get spelled out into something unrecognisable.

A bug that would have shipped a placeholder as a wave height

Placeholders are built as text with a letter-coded index and an "XX" terminator, deliberately containing no digits. When a unit immediately follows a figure, two placeholders end up adjacent. With a 26-letter index alphabet, the pattern that finds them matched greedily across both, treated the whole run as one placeholder with an out-of-range index, and left it in place unchanged.

The numeral became unrecoverable, and the advisory would have gone out with a placeholder where a wave height belonged. The index alphabet now excludes the letter X, so the pattern stops at the first terminator; an unrecognisable index returns an explicit failure rather than defaulting to zero, because substituting the first numeral wherever a corrupted placeholder appears is a wrong figure rather than a visible one.

2 · Units read, never assumed

The worst bug in the project's history, and the one that changed how everything else was written.

ORCA was reading ocean current speed and labelling it metres per second. The service was returning knots. That is a factor of about two — silently, on a value that feeds routing and search-and-rescue drift, where being twice wrong about how fast the water is moving puts a search area in the wrong place.

The cause was mundane: a global setting for wind speed units was being applied to the current speed as well. The fix is a principle:

The rule that came out of it

Never assume a unit. Read the unit the response declares, and convert. And if the conversion is not one ORCA knows to be physically meaningful, the value becomes UNAVAILABLE rather than being passed through with a hopeful label. A refusal is always better than a wrong number.

3 · The direction convention trap

Marine data contains a genuine, standardised, and deeply confusing inconsistency:

  • Wind direction is the direction the wind comes from. A "northerly" wind blows from north to south.
  • Current direction is the direction the water flows to. A "northerly" current flows towards the north.

These are opposite conventions for the same-looking number, and getting one backwards produces a field that looks completely plausible and points exactly the wrong way. Nobody notices in a screenshot. Every fisherman notices immediately, and a drift calculation would send a search in the wrong direction.

ORCA handles this in exactly one place. Both conversions go through one function that takes the convention as an explicit argument, because writing the sign flip twice is how the two end up agreeing when they should not — and that had already happened once.

4 · Finding fishing zones

Fish gather at thermal fronts — the boundaries where warm and cool water meet. The temperature gradient there concentrates nutrients, which concentrates plankton, which concentrates fish.

INCOIS publishes daily fishing advisories, but as picture maps designed for human eyes. Software cannot read a picture of a map. So ORCA re-implements the published method from the same underlying satellite measurements, and is explicit that this is a reimplementation rather than the official product.

Three front-detection methods are available, and the interface names which produced the layer you are looking at:

MethodHow it works
Sobel gradientThe classic edge-finding filter. Fast, simple, sensitive to noise.
Canny detectorSmooths first, then finds edges, then follows them. Cleaner lines.
Cayula–Cornillon SIEDSingle Image Edge Detection — purpose-built for ocean fronts. Splits a window's temperatures into two groups and only accepts a front if the split is statistically strong, the histogram genuinely has two peaks, and the pixels on each side are spatially coherent. Three tests rather than one, which is why it produces far fewer false fronts.

Zones are ranked, and — this is the honest part — the output reports which criteria could not be applied. So a missing rank reads as "not checked" rather than "poor water".

5 · The 3D sea surface

The sea view exists to answer a question a number cannot. "Significant wave height 2.64 m, limit 1.5 m" is a comparison of two decimals. An 8.2-metre boat in a 125-metre swell with a red contour ringing the crest above its gunwale is a picture, and it is the same data.

Every wave parameter comes from the forecast:

  • Height from the significant wave height.
  • Wavelength from the deep-water dispersion relation, L = gT²/2π — the physical law linking a wave's period to its length. An 8.8-second swell is therefore about 120 metres long. This is why the scene is hundreds of metres across and why the boat looks small. That is the honest picture.
  • Direction from the reported bearing, flipped — see the convention trap above.
  • Chop on top, from wind speed. Labelled as texture rather than measurement.

The boat is drawn to its real length overall and floats by evaluating the same wave formula the graphics hardware uses, sampled at bow and stern rather than at a point — which is the physical reason a longer hull rides flatter and earns a higher limit.

Where the render is gentler than the sea, and says so

The wave shape used is a Gerstner wave — a formulation where water particles move in circles, which is what gives real waves sharp crests and broad troughs. Above a steepness of 1 the formulation folds through itself and the surface turns inside out. Real waves break near that limit too, so the cap is physical rather than a graphics trick — but it is a cap, and when it engages ORCA renders a gentler sea than the forecast describes.

So the panel says so: "Drawn at 2.31 m rather than 2.64 m — the sea is worse than this render." A visual that quietly understates a dangerous sea is the one failure this feature must not have.

Two other decisions came from looking at it rather than reasoning about it. The camera was originally 37 metres up, where a 2.6-metre swell over a 125-metre wavelength is a slope of about 1 in 23 and reads as a flat tilting plane — arithmetically true and completely misleading. It is now at 4.2 metres, eye height on a small boat, where the same crests rise into the horizon and periodically hide it. And the mesh cannot resolve anything smaller than about 8 metres, which is where almost all of the sea's visual character lives — so fine ripple is added as a lighting effect rather than as geometry, without which the specular highlight lands as one soft blob and the water reads as plastic.

6 · A note on the visual treatments

ORCA has seven visual styles: standard, thermal, night-vision, radar, bathymetric, CRT and noir. They are built as colour transforms applied by the browser's compositor rather than as a shader pass over the map, and the reason is measured rather than assumed: the console draws two stacked graphics surfaces, and sampling those into a shader means reading both back from the graphics card every frame. That already produces measurable stalls in this application's own browser logs, and it would cost exactly the performance budget the frame-rate guard exists to protect.

The frame-rate guard measures the median frame time, not the average — one 400-millisecond pause while a data layer decodes would drag an average below any threshold and trip the guard on a machine that is perfectly fine. When it does trip, it turns the treatment off and says why, rather than displaying a number and doing nothing.

Part 7

What we refuse to do

These are not gaps. They are decisions, and they are as much a part of the architecture as anything we did build.

We do not…Because
…issue warnings The IMD and INCOIS do that, with legal authority and accountability that ORCA does not have. Every surface says ORCA supplements and never replaces them, and the machine-readable export is shaped to feed their systems rather than to compete with them.
…let the AI decide anything Enforced four separate ways, described in Part 3. The model explains a verdict. It never issues or softens one.
…send messages to anyone No SMS, no push, no email. ORCA has no authority to contact a fisherman, and a prototype that quietly acquires a notification channel is one that can spam a real person.
…give a single predicted position for a missing person A search area with a stated containment fraction. A coordinated search concentrating on one point because software offered one is a worse outcome than no software at all.
…let simulated data touch a safety decision SIMULATED is excluded from decision-grade evidence by the type system. Demonstration data is drawn with a hatched pattern and is never silently mixed with live measurements.
…show a legend that does not match the pixels Legends are generated from the same lookup tables that colour the image. When a visual treatment recolours the map, the legends hide themselves and say why.
…switch off encryption verification to make a connection work INCOIS's certificate chain is incomplete. The easy fix is one line that disables checking. ORCA ships the missing intermediate certificate instead and completes the chain properly.
…guess a unit Read the declared unit and convert; refuse the value if the conversion is not physically meaningful. A refusal is better than a wrong number.
…treat a refusal as a failure "No safe passage, and here is what blocks it" and "UNVERIFIABLE, and here is what was missing" are successful answers. They are given the same visual weight as a positive result, not hidden in an error state.
The claim we do make

ORCA is safety-of-life-adjacent decision support. It is not a certified marine safety system, and real deployment would need validation against historical incident data and sign-off from a maritime safety authority. We say this in the product, not just in the documentation.

Known limitations of what is built

Different from the list above. These are things that work, and whose behaviour you should understand before relying on them.

1 · The lightning veto is coarse during the monsoon

ORCA derives lightning probability from CAPE, because India has no free authoritative real-time lightning feed — the American GOES/GLM instrument does not cover India. CAPE measures thunderstorm potential, not occurrence.

During the south-west monsoon, large stretches of the Indian coast carry 2,000–4,500 joules per kilogram, which maps above the 60% veto threshold. The result is that ORCA refuses almost everything in those areas. That is defensible — the IMD does issue widespread thunderstorm warnings in monsoon — and it is also coarse. The honest reading of a lightning-only NO-GO is "thunderstorm potential is high across this whole region", not "a storm is over your boat".

The fix we know about: high CAPE with strong convective inhibition means the atmosphere is capped and storms will not fire. Gating on CIN is standard meteorological practice, ORCA already fetches CIN, and it is not yet used. This is the most valuable unbuilt thing in the system.

2 · A coarser routing grid is a more conservative router

When a route is long, the grid coarsens to stay inside the weather service's request budget. Coarsening makes ORCA more cautious, not less: one forbidden 60-kilometre cell now blocks a corridor a boat might have threaded. ORCA says so in the response when it happens, and suggests re-checking on a shorter leg.

3 · Free-tier AI provider models move without notice

At one point in development, three of four language-model providers were simultaneously unavailable for three unrelated reasons: one model was withdrawn from new users, one free tier was removed, and one was returning empty answers because it is a reasoning model that spent its entire token budget thinking. All three were fixed, but the general problem does not go away.

ORCA's mitigation is structural: four providers tried in order, a template-based fallback if all four fail, an endpoint reporting which provider actually answered, and — most importantly — the deterministic verdict does not depend on any of them. If every AI provider on Earth went down, ORCA would still compute a correct, cited GO / NO-GO.

4 · Thresholds are ours, not INCOIS's

Already stated in Part 4, repeated here because it is the limitation most likely to matter in a real deployment. The boat-class limits are seeded from peer-reviewed small-craft literature, cited per row, versioned, and displayed on every verdict. They are defensible and replaceable. They are not official.

Part 9

Glossary

Every abbreviation and technical term used in this document, alphabetically, with full forms spelled out.

A* (A-star)
A path-finding algorithm. Finds the cheapest route through a network by combining the cost already spent with an estimate of the cost remaining. ORCA uses it for sea routing.
Admissible heuristic
In path-finding, an estimate of remaining cost that never over-estimates. It is what guarantees the route found is genuinely the cheapest rather than merely a plausible one. ORCA's estimate is plain straight-line distance with no risk term, precisely to keep this guarantee.
Agent
An AI system given tools and permission to use them in a sequence it chooses, rather than only answering from memory.
AIS — Automatic Identification System
The transponder signal ships broadcast giving identity, position, course and speed. Used for collision avoidance and traffic monitoring.
AOI — Area of Interest
The geographic rectangle ORCA covers: 60°E–100°E, 0°N–25°N.
API — Application Programming Interface
A defined way for one piece of software to request information or action from another.
ASR — Automatic Speech Recognition
Converting spoken audio into text. Also called speech-to-text or STT.
Backend
The part of a system that runs on a server and does the work — fetching data, calculating, deciding.
Bathymetry
The depth and shape of the sea floor. The underwater equivalent of topography.
Bhashini
The Government of India's national language technology platform, providing translation and speech for Indian languages.
CAP — Common Alerting Protocol
An international standard format for emergency alerts, published by OASIS. India's NDMA SACHET system and the IMD publish in it. ORCA exports CAP 1.2.
CAPE — Convective Available Potential Energy
How much energy the atmosphere has available to build thunderstorms, in joules per kilogram. Measures potential, not occurrence.
Cayula–Cornillon SIED
Single Image Edge Detection. A method designed specifically for finding ocean temperature fronts in satellite imagery, using three independent statistical tests before accepting a front.
Chlorophyll-a
The green pigment in plant plankton. Satellites measure it as a proxy for how much food is in the water, which is a proxy for where fish are.
CIN — Convective Inhibition
The energy that must be overcome before a thunderstorm can form. High CAPE with high CIN means the atmosphere is "capped" and storms will not fire. ORCA fetches it but does not yet use it.
Circuit breaker
A software pattern that stops calling a failing service for a period, rather than repeatedly retrying something that is down.
CMEMS — Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service
The European Union's operational ocean data service.
Compositor
The part of a web browser that assembles the final image from layers, using the graphics card. Effects applied here are cheap; effects requiring data to be read back from the graphics card are not.
CORS — Cross-Origin Resource Sharing
Browser security rules governing whether a page from one website may fetch data from another. Relevant to why ORCA proxies NASA imagery through its own server.
deck.gl
A graphics library for drawing large data layers on maps using the computer's graphics hardware.
Deterministic
Always producing the same output from the same input, traceably. The opposite of a language model's behaviour.
Dijkstra's algorithm
A path-finding method that explores outward from the start in all directions. Slower than A* but requires no estimate. ORCA uses it in a test to verify A*'s answers.
Dispersion relation
The physical law linking a water wave's period to its wavelength. In deep water, wavelength = gravity × period² ÷ 2π.
EEZ — Exclusive Economic Zone
The sea area a country controls economically, extending 200 nautical miles from its coast.
ERDDAP
Environmental Research Division's Data Access Program. Scientific data server software; INCOIS runs one.
ETag — Entity Tag
A fingerprint a server attaches to data so a client can ask "has this changed?" and receive a small "no" instead of the whole thing again.
Frontend
The part of a system a user sees and interacts with. Runs in the browser.
Geodesic
The shortest path between two points on a curved surface. Computing distances on the Earth's actual ellipsoidal shape rather than treating coordinates as flat.
Geofence
A virtual boundary that triggers a notification when crossed or approached.
Gerstner wave
A mathematical description of water waves in which particles move in circles, producing the sharp crests and broad troughs real waves have.
GFW — Global Fishing Watch
An organisation publishing global fishing-activity data derived from vessel transponder signals.
GIBS — Global Imagery Browse Services
NASA's service for global satellite imagery. Source of ORCA's Earth textures.
GLSL — OpenGL Shading Language
The programming language for writing small programs that run on a graphics card, used for the 3D ocean surface.
H3
A system that divides the Earth into hexagonal cells at multiple sizes, useful for spatial aggregation.
Hs — Significant Wave Height
Roughly the average height of the highest third of waves, in metres. The standard measure of sea roughness.
HTTP
The protocol web browsers and servers use to talk to each other.
IAMSAR
International Aeronautical and Maritime Search and Rescue manual. The internationally agreed reference for search-and-rescue procedure, including the drift model ORCA implements.
IMBL — International Maritime Boundary Line
An agreed sea border between two countries. The India–Sri Lanka line in Palk Bay is the one that matters most to Indian fishermen.
IMD — India Meteorological Department
India's national weather service; issues official marine warnings.
INCOIS — Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services
India's ocean information agency. Publishes fishing advisories and ocean forecasts.
ISRO — Indian Space Research Organisation
India's space agency; operates the satellites behind MOSDAC's data.
Knot (kn)
One nautical mile per hour, about 1.85 km/h. The marine unit for speed.
LangGraph
A framework for building AI workflows as an explicit graph of steps rather than one large instruction.
Langfuse
A service for recording and analysing AI system behaviour over time.
Leeway
In search and rescue, an object's own movement through the water caused by wind, as distinct from being carried by the current. Has both a downwind and a sideways component.
LLM — Large Language Model
The kind of AI behind ChatGPT and similar tools. Excellent at understanding and writing language; unreliable at exact arithmetic and rule-following.
LOA — Length Overall
A boat's total length, bow to stern. Determines its safety class in ORCA.
MapLibre
An open-source mapping library. ORCA uses its globe projection.
Monte Carlo simulation
Running a calculation many times with randomly varied inputs to see the spread of possible outcomes, rather than computing one answer. ORCA runs 2,000 particles for drift.
MOSDAC
Meteorological and Oceanographic Satellite Data Archival Centre. ISRO's satellite data portal.
MRCC — Maritime Rescue Co-ordination Centre
The Coast Guard facility that co-ordinates a search and rescue operation.
Multiset
A collection that counts repeats but ignores order. ORCA verifies translated numbers as a multiset: "1.5 and 1.5" differs from "1.5", but reordering by grammar is allowed.
Nautical mile (nm)
1,852 metres. One minute of latitude.
NDMA — National Disaster Management Authority
India's disaster management body; operates the SACHET alert system.
OASIS
The standards organisation that publishes the Common Alerting Protocol.
Otsu's method
A statistical technique for finding the best threshold to split a set of values into two groups. Used inside the SIED front detector.
PFZ — Potential Fishing Zone
An advisory indicating where fish are likely to be found.
PostGIS
An extension to the PostgreSQL database adding geographic capability. ORCA uses it when present and falls back gracefully when not.
Provenance
The origin and history of a piece of data. ORCA tracks six states for every value.
Pydantic
A Python library that checks data has the right shape at the moment it is created. ORCA uses it to make invalid data impossible to construct.
r3f — React Three Fiber
A library for building 3D scenes in a web application. Used for the opening globe and the ocean surface.
Redis
A fast in-memory data store used for caching. Optional in ORCA.
SACHET
India's national public alert system, operated by the NDMA.
SAR — Search and Rescue
Operations to locate and recover people in distress at sea.
SGP4
Simplified General Perturbations model 4. The standard method for calculating where a satellite is from published orbital data. Not yet implemented in ORCA.
Shader
A small program that runs on a graphics card, calculating the position or colour of points and pixels. What draws ORCA's 3D ocean.
SIED
See Cayula–Cornillon SIED.
Sobel and Canny
Two classic image-processing filters for finding edges. Used as simpler alternatives to SIED for front detection.
SQLite
A database that is a single file requiring no server. ORCA's default, so it runs with no infrastructure.
SSE — Server-Sent Events
A web technique for a server to push a stream of updates to a browser over one connection. How ORCA streams its reasoning and its alerts.
SST — Sea Surface Temperature
The temperature of the ocean's top layer.
Staleness
Whether data is too old to trust for its particular purpose. ORCA keeps a separate expiry per variable.
Stokes drift
The slow net transport of water in the direction waves are travelling. Folded into ORCA's measured leeway coefficients rather than modelled separately.
STRtree — Sort-Tile-Recursive tree
A spatial index that groups nearby shapes so a location query can eliminate most of them cheaply before doing exact geometry.
Supervisor pattern
An AI architecture where one component decides which specialist handles each part of a task.
TLE — Two-Line Element set
The compact standard format describing a satellite's orbit. Input to SGP4.
TLS — Transport Layer Security
The encryption behind "https". Its certificate chain must be complete and verified, which is why ORCA ships a missing intermediate certificate rather than disabling checking.
Tp — Peak Wave Period
The time in seconds between successive wave crests at the dominant wave frequency. Determines wavelength via the dispersion relation.
TTS — Text-to-Speech
Converting written text into spoken audio.
Veto
In ORCA's risk engine, a hard limit that forces NO-GO regardless of the weighted index. Implemented as a separate mechanism because a weighted average has no concept of "never".
WebGL
The browser technology that lets a web page use the computer's graphics hardware. Required for ORCA's map and 3D views.
WGS84
The global standard model of the Earth's shape, used by GPS and by ORCA's distance calculations.

In one sentence

ORCA answers a fisherman's question in his own language and out loud, shows every number's source and age, computes the go / no-go with a fixed rule engine the artificial intelligence is not permitted to overrule, and says "I cannot tell" when it cannot — which is the only version of this product that is safe to ship.
